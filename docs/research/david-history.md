## The block

### Quarry and purchase

The marble came from Carrara. Analysis of three fragments from the left second toe, broken off in 1991, placed it in the Fantiscritti quarries of the Miseglia district, confirming a provenance first proposed by a technical committee in 1866 [S7].

The purchase is dated to 1465, when Agostino di Duccio bought "a very large block of bianco ordinario" at Fantiscritti; this is stated by Wikipedia on the authority of Barron 2018, which I could not read [S2]. "Bianco ordinario" is the middling grade, not "statuario" [S2].

No contemporary document names the quarry; the 1866 attribution and the 2005 analysis are the evidence [S7].

The block went by sea and river to Florence; the trip is described as "troubled" (uncited) [S13].

### The 1464 commission

The Operai of Santa Maria del Fiore were running a series of giant prophets for the "spurs" (sproni) of the cathedral's tribune buttresses, begun with Donatello's terracotta Joshua in 1410 [S2]. On 18 August 1464 they commissioned Agostino di Duccio to make a marble figure nine braccia high for one of those buttresses, at first "formed in the Roman manner from several blocks" and then from one block [S2][S17].

Agostino roughed the figure out and lost the commission in December 1466, the year Donatello died [S13][S2]. One Italian account blames the botched roughing on his assistant Bartolomeo di Pietro da Settignano, "Baccellino" [S10].

What Agostino left: Wikipedia summarises it as the beginnings of the torso, legs and feet, with some drapery and perhaps a cavity between the legs [S2 via search]. Vasari, writing in 1550 and 1568, names a "Maestro Simone da Fiesole" instead, and says he "had hacked a hole between the legs, and it was altogether misshapen and reduced to ruin" [S8]. The documents say Agostino; Vasari's Simone is a mistake of the biographer (inferred from the mismatch between [S2] and [S8]).

### The 1476 episode

On 1476 the Operai contracted Antonio Rossellino to resume the figure; the contract "was apparently rescinded" and the block then lay in the cathedral yard for another twenty-five years [S2]. Another page says Rossellino did more rough work in 1476–77 and "spoiled" it (uncited) [S13]. The Italian heritage catalogue only says he "tried to finish the work" [S3].

### The block in 1501

An inventory of 1500 describes the block as "badly blocked out and supine" ("male abozatum et sculptum", lying on its back) [S2]. The block lay in the yard of the Opera del Duomo, today the courtyard of the Museo dell'Opera del Duomo [S10].

On 2 July 1501 the Operai decided the block, which Florentines called "il gigante", should be "raised on its feet" so that masters could examine it [S2].

Its shape: the sources call it "excessively tall and narrow" ("eccessivamente alta e stretta"), full of fissures and small holes ("taroli", likened to woodworm holes), and prone to "cottura", a loss of crystal cohesion [S10]. It was said to be friable under what is now the left arm, and there were doubts it could carry a figure standing on its legs alone [S10].

Michelangelo was 26 when he got the block [S2].

## The contract and the carving

### 16 August 1501

The consuls of the Arte della Lana and the Operai assigned the "gigante" to Michelangelo on 16 August 1501 [S1][S3]. The contract calls the block "nine braccia in height" and "badly blocked", gives two years "beginning from the first day of September", and pays six gold florins a month plus a workplace at the Opera [S2]. Hirst gives the same term: "he was allowed two years to complete the marble David", so the due date was August 1503 [S4].

Vasari says the block was granted to him "by Soderini and the Wardens... as a thing of no value", that Andrea Contucci of Monte Sansovino had been seeking it, and that Michelangelo first "made a model of wax, fashioning in it, as a device for the Palace, a young David with a sling in his hand" [S8]. Condivi says only that the Operai "sent for Michael Angelo" and "in the end they offered it to him" [S11].

### 9 and 13 September 1501

A marginal note on the deliberation records that "on 9 September, he had with one or two blows of the chisel (uno vel duo ictibus) removed a certain nodus (quoddam nodum) that it had on its chest", and that he "began to work on the said giant on the morning of 13 September 1501" [S5]. Lavin reads the nodus not as a knot of drapery but as "a knob of marble deliberately retained by Agostino as a fixed reference for measuring off his colossus from the model", and notes Michelangelo removed it before the wall went up, while the block was still visible to all [S5].

The catalogue gives the same start date from the same note: "13 settembre" [S3].

### The shed

Payments survive for a wall around the block on 14 October 1501 and for its roof on 20 December 1501 (Frey 1909, nos. 12 and 13) [S5]. Hirst also notes "the completion of the 'turata' round the block in 1501 is documented" [S4]. Vasari describes "an enclosure of planks and masonry, thus surrounding the marble", inside which he worked "continuously without anyone seeing it" [S8]. The Italian Wikipedia calls it a "recinto di tavole" (board enclosure) built from 14 October 1501 [S10].

The Jervis edition of Landucci glosses the workplace as "one of the rooms of the Opera di Santa Maria del Fiore" [S6]; the payments for a wall and a roof, and Landucci's own words that the wall above the door had to be broken to get the statue out, point to a shed built in the yard rather than a room (inferred).

### February 1502

In February 1502 the Operai revised the terms. The statue was then described as "iam semifactum", already half made [S4]. The fee was set at 400 gold ducats in total ("400 ducati", "febbraio 1502") [S3]; a French summary dates the amendments to 25 and 28 February 1502 [S18]. Hirst speaks of "the extended term agreed in 1502 for its completion" and places the June 1503 showing "eight months before" it, which puts the new deadline about February 1504 (the date is inferred from his wording) [S4].

So by February 1502, about five months after the first blow, the figure was far enough advanced that the Operai called it half made and fixed a lump sum [S3][S4].

The Accademia's page attaches the 400 ducats to the 16 August 1501 assignment itself; the catalogue and Hirst attach the sum to February 1502. The documents side with February 1502 (inferred from [S3][S4] against [S1]).

### 1503: the public showing

A Deliberazione of the Operai of 16 June 1503 ordered that on the 23rd, the eve of St John the Baptist, "the door of the giant be opened and stay open the whole day so that the marble giant can be seen by all who wish to see it" ("aperiatur hostium gigantis et tota dicta die apertum sit adeo quod possit videri gigas marmoreus ab omnibus volentibus videre") [S4]. Hirst concludes the giant "must have been substantially completed by mid-summer 1503", against the older habit of saying it was finished in early 1504 [S4].

New Operai took office on 1 July 1503, among them Bartolommeo Pitti, for whom Michelangelo carved the Pitti tondo [S4].

### January 1504

On 25 January 1504 the Operai conceded the statue could not go up on the cathedral and convened a committee of about thirty citizens and artists, among them Leonardo da Vinci, Botticelli, Filippino Lippi, Perugino, Giuliano da Sangallo, Piero di Cosimo and Cosimo Rosselli; of nine proposed sites the members split closely between the ringhiera in front of the Palazzo and the Loggia [S2][S1]. One Italian account dates the meeting to 26 January and quotes the statue as "quasi finita" [S10].

Vasari's anecdote belongs to this phase: Soderini said the nose "was too thick"; Michelangelo climbed the staging, picked up "a little of the marble-dust that lay upon the planks", let it fall while pretending to work, and the Gonfalonier answered "I like it better... you have given it life" [S8].

## The placement and the move

### The late choice of the site

A Deliberazione of 30 April 1504 about moving the statue still intended it for the Loggia; only on 28 May 1504 did the Signoria decide on the place of Donatello's bronze Judith, "before the door of the Palace" [S4]. The catalogue gives deliberations of 28 and 29 May for the same decision [S3].

### 14–18 May 1504

Landucci, a druggist whose shop was on the route, wrote under 14 May 1504 (Jervis translation, p. 214) [S6]:

The marble giant "was taken out of the Opera (Office of Works of the Duomo); it was brought out at 24 in the evening (8 p.m.), and they had to break down the wall above the door so that it could come through."

"During the night stones were thrown at the giant to injure it, therefore it was necessary to keep watch over it. It went very slowly, being bound in an erect position, and suspended so that it did not touch the ground with its feet."

"There were immensely strong beams, constructed with great skill; and it took four days to reach the Piazza, arriving there on the 18th at 12 in the morning (8 a.m.). It was moved along by more than 40 men. Beneath it there were 14 greased beams, which were changed from hand to hand."

The Jervis translation then says "they laboured till the 8th July, 1504, to place it on the ringhiera, where the 'Judith' had been, which was now removed and placed inside the Palagio in the court" [S6]. Italian sources, the Opera del Duomo and the catalogue read the same entry as 8 June, which also fits the base order of 11 June (see Conflicts) [S12][S3][S2].

The translator explains the Florentine clock: the day was counted from sunset, so "the present eight o'clock was called 24" [S6].

Vasari names the engineers: "Giuliano da San Gallo and his brother Antonio made a very strong framework of wood", and they "drew it with windlasses over flat beams laid upon the ground, and then set it in place" [S8]. The Italian account calls the frame a wooden cage ("gabbia lignea") on beams greased with tallow [S10].

The Otto di Guardia records name the stone-throwers: four youths, Vincenzo di Cosimo Martelli, Filippo di Francesco de' Spini, Gerardo Maffei de' Gherardini and Raffaello di Agostino Panciatichi, all from families leaning to the Medici; three were sentenced to the Stinche prison unless they paid fines, and the records confirm Landucci that the attack came on the first night of the journey [S4]. Parenti's chronicle is the second eyewitness source [S4][S2].

The distance was about half a mile from the Opera behind the cathedral to the Piazza della Signoria [S2]; the Opera's magazine says "four hundred meters", forty men, four days [S12].

### The base

On 11 June 1504 Simone del Pollaiolo (Cronaca) and Antonio da Sangallo the Elder were ordered to build a base "subtus et circum circa pedes gigantis", a sheath around a core rather than a solid block (Weil-Garris Brandt 1983) [S2]. That sixteenth-century base was lost in the 1873 move [S2].

### 8 September 1504

Landucci: "8th September. The giant in the Piazza was finished, and completely uncovered." [S6]. The Accademia dates the public unveiling to the same day [S1]. The catalogue reads Landucci as saying Michelangelo may have kept working on the statue in the Piazza until September [S3].

### The gilded details

Three parts were once gilded: a garland on the head, the tree trunk ("broncone") behind the right leg, and the sling [S1]. Falletti, the Accademia's former director, writes that the rough texture of the sling and trunk probably helped the gold bond, and that an older partial gilding may have covered the sling, the trunk and a wreath described as "a thread of brass with twenty-eight copper leaves"; payment records show a crown existed but not whether it sat on the head or for how long, and the head shows no oxidation damage [S19]. Wikipedia, citing Lavin 1990 and Paoletti 2015, says that soon after installation the stump and sling strap were gilded and the figure received "a vine of copper leaves" and "a laurel wreath of gilt bronze"; citing Goffen 2002 it says "a gilt loin-garland and victory wreath" [S2]. The Italian Wikipedia has "brass garlands with gilded-copper leaves" circling the head and the sling strap (uncited) [S10].

Whether the 28-leaf garland went on the head or around the hips is therefore unsettled; popular pages that say it hid the genitals and came off in 1550 cite nothing [S20]. None of the gilding survives after centuries outdoors [S1][S19].

Michelangelo was paid 400 florins (Italian Wikipedia, undated, uncited) [S10]; Vasari says "four hundred crowns" from Soderini [S8]; Condivi says "four hundred ducats" and that he "finished it in eighteen months", which the documents contradict (he worked from September 1501 and the statue was substantially done by June 1503, about 21 months, and finished in 1504) [S11][S4] (the arithmetic is mine).

## Dimensions and geometry

### Height and weight

The museum gives 517 cm and 5,560 kg [S1]. The Stanford scan team, who built their gantry for the 434 cm of the books, found the statue "is not 434cm without his pedestal; he is 517cm" [S9]. Hirst's caption gives 513.5 cm "including base" [S4]. The CNR centrifuge study of 2014 used 5,572 kg [S14]. A travel site reports 5,660 kg as an estimate and notes the statue has never been weighed [S15]. Wikipedia attributes "approximately 8.5 tons" to Wallace 2017; I could not read Wallace, and I infer the figure refers to the rough block, not the finished statue [S2].

The 517 cm includes the carved rock on which the figure stands but not the museum pedestal (inferred from [S9] "without his pedestal" and from [S1]). The Italian Wikipedia line "5,17 m incluso il basamento di 108 cm" is uncited and does not fit the scan; the museum pedestal is said there to be 63 cm lower than the original base [S10].

### The block and what it forced

The only recorded dimension is the nine braccia of 1464 and 1501 [S2][S17]. At about 58 cm per braccio that is about 5.22 m [S12]; at the Florentine braccio of 58.36 cm it is 5.25 m (my arithmetic).

The width can be bounded by the statue, 199 cm [S2]. The depth is nowhere recorded. Wikipedia's Agostino page calls the block "rather shallow" and infers "almost 2 m wide and 1.1 m deep" and "over 20 tons" from the finished statue, all uncited [S13]; a dimensions database gives the statue as 1.99 m wide and 1.40 m deep, also unsourced [S16]. A measured depth of the statue from the open mesh would settle the lower bound and is a task for worker `research-model` (inferred need).

What the thin block forced is argued more than documented. Britannica suggests the slab's narrowness may explain the lean build [S21]; a guide argues the earlier cuts kept the figure from projecting beyond its base, which is why the arms stay close to the body [S22]; the Italian Wikipedia says the block was "excessively tall and narrow" and friable under the left arm [S10]. Lavin stresses the relief-like, one-face method: Michelangelo worked from a wax model and, since the wall made judging from a distance impossible, probably measured from fixed reference points, which is why he kept bits of the original block at the head and at the base [S5].

The figure sits in the block with its full height along the block's length, the raised left arm and the right hand within the block's width, and the whole composition flat enough to fit the shallow depth (inferred from [S10][S13][S22]); the stump behind the right leg uses depth that would otherwise be waste and carries load [S14][S19].

### The proportions and the buttress

The figure was meant for a buttress of the tribune, part of the prophet series on the spurs of the four diagonal buttresses of the dome [S2]. The head and the right hand are slightly enlarged so that they read from below [S10][S23]. Popular pages say the spot was "about 80 metres" up, one says over 12 metres; none cites a document, so the height is open [S20][S23]. The museum pedestal is lower than the original base, so the from-below effect is now weaker [S10].

The eyes diverge: measuring the pupil holes in the 3D model gives 21.5° (±2°) of divergence; each eye reads correctly from its own side, and the raised left arm blocks the head-on view for most viewers, which the Stanford team take as support for the idea that he "designed for the profile" [S24].

## The marble

Grade: "bianco ordinario", not "statuario" [S2]. Mineral: entirely calcite [S2]. Quarry: Fantiscritti, Miseglia, Carrara [S7].

Flaws: the block was full of "taroli", small holes, and veins [S10][S2]; a 2004 study describes the surface cavities as about a millimetre across with halos a few millimetres wide [S2]. ABC Science (2005) summarised the provenance paper as showing a poor-quality marble full of microscopic holes that may degrade faster than other Carrara marble [S25]. Historic restorers filled veins and taroli with lime mortar to restore a smooth skin [S10].

Colour and surface: the scan team describe the surface as "a complex brew of marble veining, dirt, patina, and the effects of weathering and corrosion" [S9]; the face is covered with shallow pits that some experts take for natural air pockets widened by weathering [S24]. After the 2004 cleaning, yellowish streaks on the left shin and mould discoloration on the lower back remained [S2 via search]. Beeswax lies over most of the figure, in places condensed into orange-brown beads [S19].

Weathering history: lightning struck the base on 31 August 1512 (Nardi), which chroniclers took as the first cause of the ankle cracks [S19][S10]; the statue stood outdoors until 1873 [S14]; in 1843 Costoli cleaned it with a 50 per cent hydrochloric acid solution, which one account says cut up to 2 mm into the marble in places [S2][S10]; Papi's plaster mould of 1846–47, of no fewer than 1,500 pieces, some over 680 kg, strained the structure and left plaster between the toes [S19]. Horizontal micro-fractures run across both ankles and the tree trunk; they are tied to the forward lean of about 28 cm from the centre of gravity at the old site and to the thin ankles, and the 2014 CNR study warned they threaten the stability of the statue [S19][S14][S10].

## Surface finish and tool marks

Tools: the 2003 examination recorded traces of the subbia (punch), the unghietto, the gradina (claw chisel) and a smaller tool called the dente di cane, and found "no evidence of Michelangelo using flat chisels" [S2]. A bow-driven drill shaped the hair and the pupils [S2]. Worker `research-technique` covers the kit in depth; this file only records what is visible on the statue.

Polished and smooth parts: the scan team call the surface "largely smooth overall" [S9]. The skin is finished, and the pits in it are in the stone, not tool marks [S24].

Tool marks on the finished statue: chisel cuts are visible "to the right of the sideburns" and "just ahead of the sideburn" [S9][S24]. Drill holes line the base of the hairline; the stone bridges between drilled holes were sometimes chiselled out and sometimes left [S24]. The sling strap and the tree trunk were left rough, a texture that probably helped the gilding bond [S19]. The base rock is carved roughly and carries a deep incision that may be a technical reference, plus pencil marks from the 1847 mould or the 1873 ropes [S19].

The old marble at the head and the base: Vasari says Michelangelo "suffered certain of the first marks of Maestro Simone's chisel to remain on the extremity of the marble, some of which are still to be seen" [S8]. Lavin reads Vasari and Condivi as saying bits of the original block were left "at the head and at the base of the figure", and reports from Manni's note to the 1746 edition of Condivi that the patch at the top of the head ("la scorza nella sommità del capo") was no longer to be seen, the statue having been "cleaned again" some years before, so it was removed in the eighteenth century [S5]. Whether tool marks remain on the top of the head today is not settled by any source I read; a Google Arts & Culture 360° story shows the top of the head and is the place to check [S26].

The pupils: the Stanford model shows "the pupil is a hole in the eyeball's sphere" [S24]. Many popular sources say the holes are cut as hearts, with a bridge of marble left as a highlight; The Florentine's editor calls hearts an unusual choice and the reason unknown [S27]. I found no scholarly description of the heart shape; the open detail photographs listed in `references.md` are the way to verify it (inferred).

The hair: drilled curls with deep recesses that the laser could not always reach [S24].

## Later history

The left arm was broken into three pieces by debris thrown from the Palazzo during the riot of 26 April 1527, and the sling was chipped at shoulder height; Vasari and Salviati, "although just boys", saved the fragments, and a repair possibly under Cosimo I in 1543 is suggested by a tin fragment [S2][S10][S19].

The statue was moved to the Accademia in a wheeled wooden crate on rails between 31 July and 4 August 1873 (30 July to 10 August in another account), stayed crated until the gallery opened on 22 July 1882, and lost its sixteenth-century base [S3][S2][S10].

In 1991 Piero Cannata broke the tip of the second toe of the left foot with a hammer; the fragments later served the provenance analysis; the cleaning of 2003–04, directed by Franca Falletti and done by Cinzia Parnigoni with distilled-water poultices, was the first since 1843 [S2][S7][S1].

## Conflicts and open questions

- Height: 517 cm (museum, Stanford) against 513.5 cm (Hirst's caption) and the old 434 cm; take 517 cm [S1][S9][S4].
- Weight: 5,560 kg (museum), 5,572 kg (CNR), about 5,660 kg (estimate), "8.5 tons" (Wallace via Wikipedia, probably the block); take 5,560 kg and say it is the museum's figure [S1][S14][S15][S2].
- Block depth: unrecorded; 1.1 m and 1.4 m are both unsourced inferences; the mesh can give a lower bound [S13][S16].
- The fee: six florins a month in August 1501, 400 ducats in February 1502 (catalogue, Hirst); the Accademia page attaches 400 ducats to August 1501 [S2][S3][S4][S1].
- The siting meeting: 25 January 1504 (Levine; Accademia) against 26 January (Italian Wikipedia citing Baldini) [S2][S1][S10].
- Ringhiera date: 8 June 1504 in Italian sources and the Opera's magazine; "8th July" in the 1927 English translation of Landucci; 8 June is consistent with the base order of 11 June [S12][S3][S6][S2].
- The garland: on the head (Accademia; Falletti's "crown"), around the hips (Goffen's "loin-garland"; popular pages), or both a head wreath and a vine; 28 copper leaves on a brass thread is the one number in a curatorial source [S1][S19][S2][S20].
- The buttress height: 80 m and 12 m are both unsourced [S20][S23].
- The top of the head today: old marble removed in the eighteenth century per Manni; present tool marks unverified [S5][S26].
- Heart-shaped pupils: popular and photographic, not scholarly [S27][S24].
- The 1476 Rossellino episode: "rescinded" against "spoiled it further" [S2][S13].
