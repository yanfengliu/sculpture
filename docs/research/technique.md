# The order of work: how the David was carved, stage by stage

Status: research note, 2026-10-11, worker `research-technique`. Read-only research; the decisions it asks for belong to the coordinator and are listed at the end.

This note gives the sequence of stages an animation can follow, with the tools of each stage, how the surface looks when the stage ends, a rough share of the total time, and the sources. "Stated" means a source says it; "inferred" means this note derives it and says from what. `[S n]` points to the Sources list; `[S n, summary]` means seen only through a search summary because the page would not load. Tool dimensions and marks are in `tools.md`.

## The record that anchors the timeline

Stated. On 16 August 1501 the consuls of the wool guild, the Operai of the cathedral and Michelangelo signed the contract for "a man called the Giant, nine braccia high, of marble, existing in the said Opera, once blocked out by master Agostino" ("quendam hominem vocatum gigantem abozatum, brachiorum novem ex marmore ... olim abozatum per magistrum Agostinum") [S25]. The term was two years from September, the salary six gold florins a month, and the Operai were to supply the workmen and timber [S26]. An earlier Operai deliberation of 2 July 1501 described the block as badly blocked out ("male abozatum") and lying on its back in the Opera courtyard, and ordered it raised on its feet so that masters could examine it [S3] [S29, summary].

Stated. On 9 September 1501 Michelangelo "with one or two blows of the chisel" removed "a certain knot that it had on the chest" ("uno vel duobus ictibus scarpelli substulisset quoddam nodum quem habebat in pectore"; the Bazzanti transcription prints "nisum") [S25] [S3]. On Monday 13 September 1501, early in the morning, he "began to work firmly and strongly" ("incepit firmiter et fortiter laborare") [S25].

Stated. On 28 February 1502 the valuers found the statue half finished ("iam semifactum") and fixed his whole fee at 400 gold florins, salary included [S26] [S30, summary]. On 25 January 1504 an Operai minute called the David nearly finished and a council of artists met to choose its site [S26]. Preparations for the move began on 1 April 1504; the statue left the Opera on 14 May, took four days to reach the Piazza with more than forty men on the rollers, and stood on its base by 8 June; it was unveiled on 8 September 1504 [S26] [S3]. Later that summer the sling and the tree stump were gilded [S3]. Condivi says the whole took eighteen months [S26]; the documents say twenty-eight months to "nearly finished".

Inferred calendar. 13 September 1501 to 28 February 1502 is 168 days; 28 February 1502 to 25 January 1504 is 697 days; together 865 days to "nearly finished". The half-done report therefore falls at 19 percent of the time, which is the strongest single fact about the pace: the roughing was fast and the finishing slow. Note that in 1503 and 1504 he also took the Apostles contract (April 1503), the Bruges Madonna and the tondi, so calendar months after 1502 are not full working months on the David [S31, summary]. Florentine documents date the year from 25 March; the dates above are given in modern style as the secondary sources give them.

## Stage 0: the stock, its support and the shed

Stated. The block was Carrara marble of "bianco ordinario grade stone, rather than the superior statuario", with many small cavities; the local word for cavities under a millimetre is `taroli`, for larger ones `tarme` [S3]. Agostino di Duccio had in 1464 to 1466 begun "only the trunk, lower limbs and feet"; Antonio Rossellino was engaged in 1476 and the contract rescinded; the block then lay neglected in the yard [S3]. The restorer of 2004 said "the quality of the marble is extremely poor" [S24]. Vasari says the block was spoiled by a "Maestro Simone" who cut a hole between the legs; Symonds notes that the documents name Agostino [S26] [S25]. Condivi says Michelangelo added no piece of stone, and that at the top of the head and at the base some of the block's original rough crust still shows [S26] [S32, summary].

Stated. Carving began "inside a wooden shed erected for the purpose, not far from the cathedral" [S26]; the Opera's own magazine places it in the Opera courtyard, on the site of the present museum [S33, summary]. Vasari says Michelangelo had a `turata`, an enclosure of walls and planks, built so that no one saw him work [S25]. A scaffold ("ponte") with planks stood beside the figure; Vasari's Soderini story has Michelangelo climb it with marble dust in his hand [S25] [S26]. Nine braccia is about 5.25 m (inference: Florentine braccio of 58.4 cm); the statue is 5.17 m with its base [S3].

Inferred for the animation. The block stands upright on its own carved base on a timber cradle or bed inside a plank enclosure; a two-level timber scaffold with plank decks rings the front and sides at about 1.5 m and 3.2 m; a plumb line hangs from the top beam. Nothing in the record describes the cradle or the scaffold's form, so the design follows the "workmen and timber" the Operai paid for and the scaffold Vasari mentions.

Share of time. About 2 percent: the nodo test blows on 9 September and the formal start on 13 September.

## Stage 1: laying the figure out on the stock

Stated, the model. Vasari's Life says Michelangelo made a wax model of a young David with a sling [S25]; Symonds thinks he worked from drawings and "a small wax model about eighteen inches in height" and notes two wax models at the Casa Buonarroti that do not match the statue exactly [S26]. Cellini prescribes a first model "about two palms high" (about 50 to 60 cm, inference on the Florentine palmo) and then a model at the marble's size; sculptors who cut from a small model, "Donatello among them", were less satisfied; Michelangelo "worked in both ways" and made full-size models when small ones failed him, which Cellini saw "in the Sacristy of St. Lorenzo" [S2]. Those full-size models date from the 1520s; no full-size model for the David is recorded. The Accademia's commentators say he carved freehand, from the front toward the back, without a plaster model marking the cuts [S9, summary] [S12].

Stated, transferring the model. Cellini: "draw the principal views of your statue on to the stone" with care, since a bad drawing can ruin the block [S2]. Vasari: set a carpenter's square against the block with one leg horizontal at the foot of the figure and the other upright, and a matching square on the model, to carry the projection of legs and arms across [S1]. The 1907 editor adds Alberti's `definitor` and later plumb-line frames and names the pointing machine as the modern aid [S1]; the pointing machine is therefore excluded.

Stated, where to start. Vasari's rule: the figure should come out of the block in measured stages, "in the same manner that one would lift a wax figure out of a pail of water, evenly and in a horizontal position", so the highest parts appear first [S1] [S14]. Cellini: once the principal view is drawn, cut around it "as if you wanted to work a half relief", so the figure "gradually emerges"; this is "the right method, and the one which the great Michaelangelo employed"; those who cut at scattered points to save time "took all the longer in the end, and wasn't near so good" [S2]. Vasari warns against those who "rashly cut away the marble in front and at the back", leaving no way back and forcing patched-on pieces "after the fashion of cobblers" [S1]. On St Matthew the most projecting parts, the left knee and a hand, were cut first, and the carving advanced from the front face toward the back without moving round the block [S14].

Inferred for the animation. The front face of the block carries the drawn principal view of the David (chalk or charcoal, a single contour with the main masses); the sides carry the side contours; the plumb line and the square check the heights of knee, hip, hand, shoulder and chin against the small wax model. The first cut after the nodo opens the front face around the contour.

Share of time. About 1 percent, overlapping the start of roughing.

## Stage 2: roughing out with the point

Tools. The heavy `subbia` with the heavy iron `mazzuolo`; wedges only if a corner must come off whole.

Stated. The point is the "workhorse" and removes at least 85 percent of the stone [S4]. On marble it is driven at 90 degrees for bulk removal, which leaves a pitted surface, at about 70 degrees for short grooves, and at about 45 degrees without lifting between strokes to cut "a series of more controlled parallel lines" that outline the form [S4]. Cellini: work with the `subbia` "to within at least half a finger" of "the last skin but one" [S2]. Vigenère saw chips three or four fingers thick come off with one blow, exactly at the mark [S7, summary]. The Operai's "firmiter et fortiter" of 13 September matches heavy point work [S25].

Where the sculptor starts. Stated: the highest points of the front, the chest where the nodo stood, the knee, the hand (by Vasari's water rule and the St Matthew evidence) [S1] [S14]. Inferred: on the David, the left knee and the right hand hang furthest forward and are found first, then the chest and face, with the back still square block until the front is a relief about a hand deep.

Surface at the end. Inferred: a faceted figure in parallel point furrows, 10 to 30 mm proud of the final surface everywhere on the front, the bounds being Cellini's half finger (about 10 mm) and a modern guide's 2 to 3 cm [S2] [S8, summary]; the back and the sides still show saw-flat or quarry-rough block with the old 1460s roughing of Agostino at the legs and feet.

Share of time. About 12 percent (inferred: roughly the first three and a half months).

## Stage 3: the calcagnuolo and the coarse claw

Tools. The two-tooth `calcagnuolo`, then the three-tooth `gradina`, with the iron mallet.

Stated. Vasari: after the point come the `calcagnuoli` "which have a notch in the middle of the edge", then "finer and finer tools with more teeth"; the `gradina` is "worked over the whole figure to keep muscle and drapery in proportion" [S1]. On St Matthew the parts not yet reached were "carved coarsely" with the `calcagnuolo`, and the knee was brought up through all three chisels [S14]. The David's 2003 traces include the `subbia` and the `gradina` [S3].

Surface at the end. Inferred: the point furrows are combed down to 5 to 10 mm proud after the `calcagnuolo` and 2 to 5 mm after the coarse claw, the whole front now reading as the figure in rounded masses; the measured 1 to 5 mm trace depth on St Matthew bounds the claw stage [S11]. The half-done valuation of 28 February 1502 is taken as the end of this stage.

Share of time. About 7 percent to the half-done report (inferred); stages 0 to 3 together are the documented 19 percent.

## Stage 4: refining with the fine claw, the roundel and the sides and back

Tools. Fine `gradina` and `dente di cane`, `unghietto`; the flat `scalpello` little or not at all on the David [S3]; lighter mallet.

Stated. The `gradina`'s teeth give the surface a pleasing texture, and a smooth chisel then erases the tooth marks in Vasari's general sequence [S1]; the 2003 study found no flat-chisel evidence on the David, and the unfinished works reach their state "almost entirely with toothed chisels" [S3]. The claw "works around the form" and lays fine cross-hatching over the point grooves [S10]. The roundel serves hollows, hair and beards [S4]; `unghietto` traces are on the David [S3]. The sides and back are reached last, in the same order of tools, which is why the Prisoners' backs are the least worked [S12] [S14].

Surface at the end. Inferred: the whole figure within 1 to 2 mm of its final skin, covered in fine crossing claw bands under 1 mm deep; hollows of the hair, the eyes and the hand scooped with the roundel; the back and sides brought level with the front.

Share of time. About 37 percent (inferred: most of 1502 and the working months of 1503).

## Stage 5: drilling

Tools. The `trapano a violino` (bow drill) with a cord or bow, a wooden guide for running cuts, the `trapano a petto` for deep holes [S2] [S18]; a narrow chisel to knock out the webbing [S18].

Stated. Cellini: drills undercut difficult drapery and free-standing poses; the finer drill turned with a cord is for fine hair and drapery [S2]. On the David the pupils and the hair were worked with the bow drill [S3]; the scan of the head shows "drill holes along the bottom of the hairline", and "sometimes the webbing between drill holes was knocked out with a chisel, sometimes not" [S18]; drill marks show under the forehead curls on a faithful cast [S20]. The running drill is driven in at 35 to 45 degrees, lifted, shifted and repeated to make a channel [S4]. The Taddei Tondo has no drilling at all [S15].

Inferred for the David. Drilling after the fine claw and before the rasps, at the hairline and between the curls, the pupils, the nostrils, the gap between the sling and the shoulder, between the fingers of the left hand, around the stump and between the legs where the chisel cannot reach.

Share of time. About 8 percent (inferred).

## Stage 6: rasps and files

Tools. Iron rasps straight and curved, curved files, slender files, rifflers [S1] [S2].

Stated. Vasari: the rasps remove the tooth marks; the files soften the surface further [S1]. The rasp leaves "fine scratches crisscrossing the surface" [S4]. The Atlas lists a rasp among its tools and the Awakening Slave shows chisel and rasp marks together [S21, summary] [S12].

Surface at the end. Inferred: claw bands gone from the flesh, hair and drapery still keeping chisel texture; a dull, even skin of crossing scratches under half a millimetre deep.

Share of time. About 12 percent (inferred).

## Stage 7: abrasives and polish

Tools and materials. Pumice "points" worked wet, tripoli earth on leather, straw bunches, leather [S1] [S2]; emery is named by Vasari for porphyry only [S1].

Stated. Vasari: pumice gives "a flesh-like appearance", tripoli adds lustre, straw bunches give the final shine [S1]. Cellini: finish with "a fine white, close-grained pumice stone" [S2]. The David was polished when finished, unlike the Taddei Tondo [S15]; after the 2004 cleaning "the original shine of the marble had even returned" in places [S24]. The polishing of a large figure is "extremely labour intensive" [S4].

Surface at the end. Inferred: a satin sheen on flesh, a matt pumice skin where tooth texture is kept (hair, sling, stump), no mirror gloss.

Share of time. About 15 percent (inferred), ending at the "nearly finished" minute of 25 January 1504.

## Stage 8: on site

Stated. The statue stood on its base by 8 June 1504 with the scaffolding still up when Soderini criticised the nose [S26]; the sling and stump were gilded that summer and the museum records a gilded garland for the head [S3] [S32, summary]; the unveiling was 8 September [S3].

Inferred. Final touches on the nose, hair and base, and the gilding, belong to the last frames; the gilding is a documented feature that must arrive through a tool (a brush and leaf), not appear.

## What Michelangelo left unpolished, and why

Stated. Condivi: the block's rough crust still shows at the top of the head and at the base, offered as proof that he fitted the figure to the stone without adding pieces [S26] [S32, summary]. The drill holes at the hairline and the webbing between them were left [S18]. On finished and nearly finished statues, "especially those in the Medici Chapel", Michelangelo "often left the surface deliberately bumpy", and the tiny shadows "deepen the shading of curved surfaces" [S11]. St Matthew, the four Prisoners, the Rondanini Pietà and the Taddei Tondo were left at their working stages, which is why they are the evidence for this note [S14] [S15] [S34, summary]. The Taddei Tondo's unfinished state has been read as an "expressive device" and as a decision to show the finished parts to advantage [S15].

Inferred. On the David the choice is practical as much as aesthetic: the figure was made for a height, "large statues can only be seen from below" and he "designed his statues accordingly" [S11], so the top of the head and the back of the hair needed less finish; the hair and the sling keep tooth texture to read against the polished flesh; and the block's shallowness left no margin at the crown.

## How marble behaves under the tools

Stated, the stone. Carrara marble is a mosaic of interlocking calcite crystals, formed from limestone by metamorphism [S35]; calcite is soft (Mohs 3) with three cleavage planes, so a blow in line with a crystal's cleavage splits it cleanly while one across it breaks it unevenly [S36, summary] [S35]. Cellini grades marbles from a coarse, bright-specked kind that is "the hardest to carve", where delicate parts risk cracking, to a soft, skin-toned kind, "the most cohesive, the most beautiful and the tenderest marble in the world to work in"; Carrara blocks with dark stains wear down tools, and "they deceive you with a lovely surface outside, while within are all these blots" [S2]. Vasari: veins "called by the sculptors smerigli (emery veins)" usually cause the tools to break [S1].

Stated, chips and dust. One expert blow brings off chips "three or four fingers" thick [S7, summary]. The point driven at a shallow angle lets the chip break away sideways; driven straight in it leaves a pit and hidden damage [S6, summary]. The dust is white and fine; Vasari's Soderini anecdote has a handful of it lying on the scaffold planks [S25]. The furrow bottoms stay whiter than the surrounding marble "due to the crushing of marble crystals under the impact of the chisel" [S11].

Stated, bruising. Bruises are "clouds of microscopic cracks in the uniformly crystallized marble" left by a hard strike; older texts call them "stunning" or "shocking"; they are "indelible milky spots" and "the more you polish, the more that spot will show". In Peter Coates's tests a punch left clouds "to a depth of about 1/4 inch below the deepest point under the punctures", shaving away 3/8 inch did not reach the bottom of one bruise, a five-tooth claw bruised more shallowly because the force was spread, and blows along the stone's banding bruised deeper than blows across it [S37]. Cellini's rule that the fine point is not "set straight in" so the marble does not crack is the period version [S2].

Stated, flaws. `Taroli` are cavities under a millimetre, `tarme` several millimetres or larger, from the stone's structure rather than decay [S3]. `Peli` are the fissures of the marble beds, three main systems (del verso, del secondo, del contro) [S38, summary]; quarry language adds `peli furbi` and `peli chiusi` (also `fili`, `sedute`, `scarpine`), `lucciche` (pores filled with crystallised calcite), `salici` (vertical mineral streaks), `tabacchi` (rust spots from pyrite) and `marmo cotto`, an altered milky and friable zone [S39, summary]. Michelangelo's contracts asked for marble "white, without defects, that is without internal cracks, without veins", "alive" and not "cooked" [S40], and for columns "privo di peli" [S39, summary]. A chisel blow on the back of the Taddei Tondo "resulted in a hairline crack" in the Virgin's face [S15]; a vein in the marble plagued the Florentine Pietà and figures among the reasons he broke it [S41].

Why angles matter, inferred from the above. At 90 degrees the energy goes into the stone as a crushed pit and a bruise cloud 6 mm or more deep; at 30 to 45 degrees the same energy shears a chip off sideways and the bruise stays in the chip; the claw spreads the force over its teeth and bruises least; an animation should therefore show the point lying low in the sculptor's stroke, chips flying forward and sideways, and only the first bulk cuts struck steeply.

## What is known of the David's surfaces

Stated. The 2003 examination found traces of the `subbia`, the `gradina`, a smaller-toothed `dente di cane` and an `unghietto`, and no flat-chisel evidence [S3]. The head keeps "chisel marks in front of the sideburn and the drill holes along the bottom of the hairline"; the face carries "shallow pits visible almost everywhere", which "some experts" take for natural air holes in the marble [S18]. The scan was made at 0.29 mm spacing, chosen because chisel marks need about 0.25 mm; the furrowed brow is "an anatomically impossible protrusion of marble" [S11] [S42]. The statue measures 5.17 m [S3]; the Stanford scan found it "considerably taller" than the published 4.34 m [S3]. The knot on the chest was the first thing removed [S25]; a later reading suggests the "nodum" may have been a reference point rather than drapery [S3]. The old crust remains at the crown and the base [S26]. The tree stump behind the right leg, called a `broncone` in a contemporary document, and the sling were gilded in 1504 [S3].

Stated, later skins. A beeswax-based coat was applied in 1813 and removed with a 50 percent hydrochloric acid solution in 1843 [S3]; a 2004 report describes an acid "dousing" after the 1873 move [S24]. The 2003 to 2004 cleaning used poultices of cellulose, clay and distilled water wrapped in rice paper for 15 to 30 minutes [S24]; "most of the cleaning involved distilled water", and the restorer filled "considerable pitting on the marble's surface" with plaster [S3]. The sheen came back "in some areas" [S24]. So the present skin is not everywhere Michelangelo's.

Inferred for the animation. Flesh: satin polish. Hair, sling, stump: tooth and roundel texture with drill holes at the hairline. Crown: a coarse crust. Base: rough. Face: natural pits, not tool marks.

## Facts the record does not settle

1. The model. Wax model of about 45 cm (Vasari, Symonds) or a full-size model (Cellini's rule, documented for Michelangelo only from the 1520s) [S2] [S26].
2. The transfer method. Drawn principal view plus carpenter's square and plumb line (Vasari, Cellini) or by eye alone (the Accademia's "freehand") [S1] [S2] [S9, summary]. The pointing machine is excluded either way.
3. The flat chisel. Vasari's general sequence includes it; the 2003 David study found no evidence of it [S1] [S3].
4. The nodo of 9 September 1501. A knot of drapery, a knob of stock left by Agostino, or a reference point; the transcriptions differ ("nodum" / "nisum") [S25] [S3].
5. The original polish. "Polished when finished" [S15] and "shine returned in places" [S24], but the 1843 acid and the 1813 wax changed the skin [S3].
6. Drill bit sizes and the exact drilling of hair and pupils: no measurement exists [S18] [S4].
7. The mallet. Iron or wood, and its weight; only modern ranges exist [S4] [S13, summary].
8. The shed and the scaffold: their form, and whether the block stood on its own base or in a cradle [S26] [S25].
9. Time per stage. Only two anchor documents exist (half done at 19 percent, nearly finished at 100 percent), and the David shared 1503 and 1504 with other commissions [S26] [S31, summary].
10. Chip size per blow on the David. Vigenère's figures describe the 1550s, not 1501 [S7, summary].
11. Whether Michelangelo forged or tempered his own chisels [S3].
12. The 1503 showing of the statue, argued by Hirst, which bears on how private the shed was [S31, summary].
13. The statue's weight: about 6 to 7 tonnes in some accounts and 8.5 in others [S25] [S3]; the history worker should settle it.
14. Where the first furrows went after the nodo: the chest, or the knee and hand as the highest points [S1] [S14].
15. The block's depth from front to back; Condivi's crust at the crown implies little margin [S26].
