# The Florentine marble sculptor's tool kit, as Michelangelo used it

Status: research note, 2026-10-11, worker `research-technique`. Read-only research; nothing here is a decision.

This note describes each tool of a Renaissance Florentine marble carver well enough for a 3D worker to model it and animate its marks. Every claim says where it comes from. "Stated" means a source says it. "Inferred" means this note derives it and says from what. Period dimensions are rarely documented, so modern hand-tool figures stand in and are marked as inference. Numbered references point to the Sources list at the end; `[S n]` means read directly, `[S n, summary]` means seen only through a search summary because the page would not load.

## The kit at a glance

The period sequence is fixed by two Florentine authors who knew Michelangelo's practice. Vasari (1550/1568) lists, in order: the `subbia` (point) to rough out, the `calcagnuoli` (notched chisels), finer toothed tools, the `gradina` (broader toothed chisel) to reduce and refine, iron rasps straight and curved to remove tooth marks, then pumice, "chalk from Tripoli", leather and wisps of straw to polish; drills "of different sizes" make every hole [S1]. Cellini (1568) gives the same spine: `subbia`, a notched `scarpello`, `lima raspa` (coarse file), `trapani` (drills), pumice [S2].

The 2003 examination of the David found traces of the `subbia`, the `gradina`, a finer-toothed `dente di cane`, and an `unghietto`, and no evidence of a flat chisel [S3]. That gives the David's own working kit: point, two-tooth, claw, fine claw, roundel, drill, plus mallet, rasps and abrasives.

Vasari's plate of tools "in actual use in a stone-cutter's yard at Settignano" (the editor's 1907 photograph) labels: E `subbia`, a point; F `calcagnuolo`, a toothed chisel; G `gradina`, a broader toothed chisel; H `scarpello`, a chisel; J `trapano`, a drill [S1].

Modern reference dimensions used below come from Wootton, Russell and Rockwell's essay on stoneworking tools, built on Peter Rockwell's practice and archive [S4]. They are the documented modern equivalents the assignment asks for, not period measurements.

## Subbia: the point (English: point, punch)

Names. Italian `subbia` (Vasari, Cellini), also `punta`. English point. The punch proper is a related tool with a wider edge, "usually 0.5 cm long", for softer stone [S4]; on marble the Florentine tool is the point.

What it is. Vasari: a tool "pointed like a stake in facets", "heavier or lighter as the case may be" [S1]; in the sculpture chapter simply "pointed and heavy" [S1]. Cellini: a fine-pointed chisel with a slim shank, and a finer `subbia` that "removes material lightly when not set straight in, which keeps the marble from cracking" [S2]. Material in the period: a wrought-iron bar with a steeled, tempered tip; Vasari does not say so, the inference is from his word "iron rasps" and the plate [S1]. Modern: tool steel, carbide-tipped only for granite [S4].

Dimensions. Stated (modern): shaft 20 to 30 cm long, 1 to 2.5 cm across, ending in a pyramidal tip; the tip is longer for marble and limestone and blunter for granite [S4]. Catalogue points run about 250 to 260 mm [S5, summary]. Inferred for the David: a heavy roughing point about 25 to 30 cm long on a 16 to 20 mm square or octagonal shank, and a finer point about 20 cm long on a 10 to 12 mm shank, both with a four-faceted pyramid tip of 30 to 40 mm. The two sizes follow Vasari's "heavier or lighter" and Cellini's "finer subbia".

How held and struck. Stated: on marble the point is held at 90 degrees for rough removal and squaring, which leaves a pitted surface; at about 70 degrees and lifted between blows ("mason's stroke") it cuts short grooves; at about 45 degrees and not lifted between strokes ("sculptor's stroke") it cuts "a series of more controlled parallel lines" that outline a form [S4]. Practice guides give 30 to 45 degrees so the chip breaks away sideways, and warn that a blow straight down leaves a pit, hidden damage and sometimes a crack that shows days later [S6, summary]. Cellini says the same in period words: the fine point is not "set straight in" [S2]. Rhythm: no source gives a count. Inferred: one to two blows a second with the mallet, the point sliding forward along its furrow between blows in the sculptor's stroke.

What it removes. Stated: "at least 85% of the stone removed in the process of carving a statue is cut away using this tool" [S4]. An eyewitness, Blaise de Vigenère, saw Michelangelo past sixty bring down with one blow chips "three or four fingers" thick, exactly at the mark, and more of them in a quarter hour than three young masons in three or four times as long [S7, summary]. Three or four fingers is about 6 to 8 cm (inference: a finger as 1.8 to 2 cm). Where it stops: Cellini says work with the `subbia` "to within at least half a finger" of the `penultima pelle`, "the last skin but one" [S2], about 9 to 10 mm (inference on the finger). A modern Italian carver's guide puts the point's stopping margin at 2 to 3 cm from the final surface [S8, summary].

The mark. Stated: deep parallel furrows [S9, summary]; "the point chisel left deep parallel grooves" [S10]. The furrow bottoms are whiter than the marble around them "due to the crushing of marble crystals under the impact of the chisel" [S11]. On St Matthew all the chisel traces together measure 2 to 10 mm wide and 1 to 5 mm deep [S11, citing Giovannini 1999]. Inferred for a 3D reproduction of heavy roughing: V-shaped furrows 10 to 25 mm wide and 5 to 15 mm deep, 15 to 40 mm apart, running in diagonal bands 10 to 30 cm long, each band at one angle, the next band crossing it; where the point was driven at 90 degrees the field is a pitted, crater-like crust with 10 to 20 mm pits. The St Matthew numbers are the measured lower bound; the roughing figures come from the Prisoners' photographs and from Vigenère's chip size.

Evidence images. St Matthew, Accademia: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Saint_Matthew_by_Michelangelo_Buonarroti (point furrows across the whole block face; the knee and head are the clearest). Awakening Slave: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Awakening_Slave_by_Michelangelo_Buonarroti (chisel scoring "especially towards where he would have been working when finishing up from behind" [S12]). Atlas: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Atlas_by_Michelangelo_Buonarroti. Taddei Tondo background: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Taddei_Tondo ("point chisel gouges", and "a smaller point made the parallel lines next to the Virgin's upper arm" [S10]).

## Calcagnuolo: the two-tooth chisel

Names. Italian `calcagnuolo` or `calcagnolo` (from `calcagno`, heel). Some modern Italian sources also call a two-tooth chisel `dente di cane`, dog's tooth [S13, summary]; the 2003 David study uses `dente di cane` for a chisel with smaller teeth than the `gradina` [S3], so the two names are not the same tool there.

What it is. Vasari: chisels "which have a notch in the middle of the edge of the blade" [S1]; "short", with "a notch in the middle", used after the point to round the form [S1]. Cellini calls his notched chisel `scarpello a tacca`, "a notch in the middle of it", and uses it to carry the work toward the file stage [S2]. Wikipedia's technique summary calls it "the two-toothed shorter blade" [S3]. The teeth are the two halves of a flat edge left either side of the notch, so each tooth is a short flat edge, not a point.

Dimensions. No period figures. Stated (modern): tooth chisels have shafts 16 to 22 cm long and 1 to 2 cm across [S4]; two-tooth heads in catalogues run from 6 mm to about 14 mm wide [S5, summary]. Inferred for the David: shank 20 cm by 12 to 14 mm, edge 15 to 20 mm wide, two flat teeth 5 to 7 mm wide with a 4 to 6 mm notch.

How held and struck. Stated: toothed chisels are "held at 35-60 degrees to the surface of the stone"; a steeper angle cuts deeper but less regularly [S4]. Struck with the iron mallet; Vasari's order puts it right after the point.

The mark. Stated: on St Matthew's head, "the large slots with in the middle the print of a tooth of the chisel, the calcagnuolo, are still clearly visible" [S14]. Inferred for 3D: pairs of parallel grooves 5 to 7 mm wide and 2 to 4 mm deep with a 4 to 6 mm standing ridge between them, each stroke 20 to 50 mm long, set side by side so that successive pairs read as a broad combed band; the ridge between the two grooves is what the eye reads as the tooth print.

Evidence images. St Matthew head (left side of the face, not yet reached) in the Commons category above [S14]. Young Slave: "wide-toothed chisel tracks remain on the rock from which the figure seems to rise" [S9, summary]; images under https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Prisoners_by_Michelangelo_Buonarroti.

## Gradina: the claw or tooth chisel

Names. Italian `gradina` (from `gradino`, step: it works in steps). English claw chisel, tooth chisel. Michelangelo's most important tool according to the Accademia's commentators [S14].

What it is. Vasari: "another chisel called gradina, used to reduce and refine the figures"; it is slimmer and flatter than the `calcagnuolo` and has "two notches", so three teeth; it is worked over the whole figure to keep muscles and drapery in proportion, and its teeth give the surface a pleasing texture [S1]. Wikipedia: "a three-toothed tool, a serrated claw chisel whose marks are seen in his unfinished sculptures" [S3]. Modern claws have three to five teeth, two-tooth versions exist [S4]; Italian catalogues sell `gradine` with two to six teeth [S13, summary].

Dimensions. Stated (modern): shaft 16 to 22 cm long, 1 to 2 cm across; edge typically 0.5 to 10 cm wide; teeth pointed or squared, which changes the mark [S4]. A forged marble set lists a 12/15 mm claw with four teeth [S5, summary]. Inferred for the David: a coarse `gradina` 20 cm by 12 mm with three squared teeth on a 15 to 18 mm edge, and a fine `gradina` (the 2003 `dente di cane`) 18 cm by 10 mm with four or five small teeth on a 10 to 12 mm edge.

How held and struck. Stated: 35 to 60 degrees; shallow angle for the regular surface, steeper for depth [S4]. It "works around the form" and is "more caressing and less penetrating than the point" [S10]. On the Taddei Tondo the point and claw were "often driven hard and with great energy" [S15, citing Larson 1991]. Struck with the iron mallet, or a wooden one for fine passes [S4].

What it removes. Inferred: 1 to 3 mm per pass over a band the width of the edge; the stated depth of St Matthew's traces, 1 to 5 mm [S11], bounds it.

The mark. Stated: fine cross-hatched lines laid over the point grooves [S10]; sets of shallow parallel lines, less regular when the chisel is steep [S4]. On St Matthew the upper neck "was carved with a gradina" and the scan shows it [S11]. Inferred for 3D: bands of three or four parallel scratches, each tooth groove 2 to 4 mm wide and 1 to 2 mm deep, band width equal to the edge (12 to 18 mm), strokes 20 to 60 mm long, successive passes crossing the previous at 30 to 90 degrees so the field reads as cross-hatching; on finished passes the grooves are under 1 mm deep and read as a soft comb texture.

Evidence images. St Matthew knee: the "far protruding left knee" was carved out "directly by using all three types of chisels, including the gradina" [S14]. Taddei Tondo (point and claw only) [S15]. Medici Chapel figures, where the surface is "deliberately bumpy" so "the tiny shadows cast by these bumps deepen the shading" [S11]. David hair and sling straps are said to keep crisp tooth marks [S16, summary; gallery blog, weak].

## Scalpello or scarpello: the flat chisel

Names. Italian `scalpello` (Vasari's plate: `scarpello`). English flat chisel; the widest are bolsters or droves.

What it is. Vasari: a "smooth chisel" that erases the marks the `gradina`'s teeth leave [S1]. Cellini's glossary: `scarpello`, a general term for chisels and cutters [S2]. Modern: shaft 15 to 25 cm long, 1 to 2 cm across, edge 0.5 to 10 cm, corners rounded for delicate work or squared for detail and lettering [S4].

How held and struck. 35 to 60 degrees; a gentle stroke smooths flat planes [S4]. The mark is "almost smooth sets of parallel straight lines", sometimes barely visible [S4].

Michelangelo and the flat chisel. Stated: the 2003 examination found "no evidence of Michelangelo using flat chisels in the work" on the David, and his pieces reached the non finito "almost entirely with toothed chisels" [S3]. A sculptor's lecture places a flat chisel after the claw on the tondi "to remove tool marks and prepare the surface for abrasion" [S10], and a travel blog reports flat passes on the Bearded Slave's face [S17, summary; unverified]. Vasari's standard sequence includes the smooth chisel [S1]. The conflict is recorded in technique.md as unsettled; the David's own evidence says little or none.

## Ugnetto or unghietto: the roundel

Names. Italian `unghietto`, `ugnetto` (little fingernail). English roundel, rondel, bullnose or round-nosed chisel.

What it is. Stated (modern): a chisel with the flat chisel's shaft (15 to 25 cm, 1 to 2 cm) and a curved edge "from a semicircle to a barely discernible curve"; used for hollows, hair and beards and natural textures; common on marble; struck with a metal hammer or wooden mallet; held at 35 to 60 degrees; its mark keeps the curve of its edge [S4]. Italian descriptions: a flattened steel-tipped chisel, longer and narrower than the point, for recesses and undercuts; a 9 mm size is sold today [S13, summary]. On Bernini's `Verità` the back shows the sequence `subbia`, `gradine`, `scalpello`, `ferrotondo`, `raspa`, `unghietto`, `trapano`, abrasives [S13, summary], so it was also used late, after the rasp, to cut details.

Michelangelo. The 2003 David study lists `unghietto` traces [S3]. Inferred use on the David: the channels of the hair, the inner corners of the eyes, the spaces between fingers and toes, the stump's bark.

The mark. Inferred for 3D: shallow scooped channels of round section, 6 to 12 mm wide and 1 to 3 mm deep, no corner lines, each stroke 10 to 30 mm long.

## Trapano: the drill (bow, strap, running and breast drills)

Names. Italian `trapano`; the bow drill is `trapano ad arco` or `violino` [S18]; Cellini's large drill is the `trapano a petto`, breast drill [S2]. English bow drill, strap or cord drill, running drill, breast drill.

What it is. Vasari: "all the drill-holes are made with drills of different sizes weighing from twelve pounds each even to twenty, according to the size of the hole needed, and they serve to finish every sort of work" [S1]. Twelve to twenty Florentine pounds is about 4 to 7 kg (inference: libbra of about 339 g); the weight must include the whole apparatus or its loading weight, not the bit. Cellini: drills undercut difficult drapery and free-standing poses; "the finer kind is turned with a cord" and serves fine hair and drapery; the larger `trapano a petto` reaches where the finer cannot [S2]. The Stanford scanning team describes the David's drill: "a sharp metal bit spun back and forth with the aid of a strung bow", a tool "often used by sculptors to create deep recesses", called `violino` [S18]. The Roman strap drill needs two workers, one pulling the cord, one steering the bit and pressing; the running drill is the same tool steadied with a wooden guide and driven in at 35 to 45 degrees, then lifted, shifted and repeated so a row of holes becomes a channel [S4]. Surviving 20th-century Tuscan `violino` drills stand 21 to 22 cm high [S13, summary].

Dimensions. No period bit sizes. Inferred: bits 3 to 8 mm for pupils, nostrils and hair channels, 10 to 15 mm for undercuts; the bit is a chisel-shaped spade point, not a twist bit [S4]; bow about 60 cm.

Michelangelo and the drill. Stated: the David's "pupils of his eyes" and the hair were worked with "the trapano, a drill worked with a bow" [S3]. The sheet in the Louvre carrying the arm of the marble David and the line "Davicte cholla fromba e io chollarcho" is read as "David with the sling and I with the bow", the bow being the drill [S3] [S19, summary]. On the David's head: "drill holes along the bottom of the hairline"; "sometimes the webbing between drill holes was knocked out with a chisel, sometimes not" [S18]. On a faithful plaster cast "under the curls on his forehead, one can see the marks left by Michelangelo's drills" [S20]. The Atlas keeps bow-drill traces along the top edge of the back [S21, summary]. The Taddei Tondo has no drill work at all, which gives its "surface unity" [S15]. The Battle of the Centaurs is called his first work made without a bow drill [S3], so he had used one before 1492.

The mark. Stated: rows of holes, joined rows cleared with a narrow chisel into a channel, single holes for pupils, nostrils and hair depth [S4]; on the David the webbing between neighbouring holes is sometimes left [S18]. Inferred for 3D: cylindrical holes 4 to 6 mm across and 8 to 20 mm deep along the hairline, hole centres 6 to 10 mm apart, some bridged by thin webs, some cleared into a channel.

## Mazzuolo: the mallet

Names. Italian `mazzuolo`, also `mazzetta`; Cellini's glossary lists `mazzuolo`, a mallet, without describing it [S2]. English mallet or hammer.

What it is. Stated (modern): "all of these chisels are struck with a hammer"; the point and tooth chisel usually with a metal hammer, sometimes wood; the flat chisel with metal, wood occasionally; the roundel with either. Italian marble hammers "usually have a square head and a relatively short handle, 15-20 cm long"; wooden mallets have "larger, rounded heads with handles of 15-20 cm" [S4]. Weights: modern Italian catalogues sell square iron `mazzuoli` of 400 g, 750 g and 1 kg and a 500 g round brass one; a teaching page gives 500 to 1,500 g chosen by the carver's habit and the job; sets pair chisels with 600 to 750 g hammers; a heavy 5 kg mallet splits big chunks off a block [S5, summary] [S13, summary]. Period image: the relief under Nanni di Banco's Four Crowned Saints at Orsanmichele (about 1410 to 1417) shows a Florentine carver striking a chisel with a short-handled hammer [S14] [S22].

Inferred for the David. A 1 to 1.5 kg iron `mazzuolo` with a square or barrel head about 40 by 40 by 80 mm on a 15 to 20 cm handle for the point and the `calcagnuolo`; a 500 to 800 g head for the `gradina`, roundel and flat passes; a wooden mallet is possible for the finest claw work and is a choice to record. Rhythm: no source; inferred one to two blows a second, slower and heavier on the point, faster and lighter on the claw.

## Raspe and lime: rasps, files and rifflers

Names. Italian `raspa`, `lima`; Cellini's `lima raspa` or `scuffina` is the coarse file; his glossary adds `lima a coltella` (knife file) and `lima mezza tonda` (half-round) [S2]. English rasp, file, riffler (the small double-ended rasp).

What it is. Vasari: "the tooth marks left in the marble are removed with iron rasps straight and curved" [S1]; in the sculpture chapter "curved files" remove the remaining `gradina` traces, then "slender files" and "straight rasps" refine [S1]. Cellini: the `lima raspa` comes in many shapes and about half a dozen sizes [S2]. Modern double-ended rifflers run 12 to 40 cm, with points cut into flattened ends, square, round or pointed, point density set by the fineness wanted; they are mainly for marble [S4].

How used and the mark. Stated: worked across the surface after chisels; the mark is "fine scratches crisscrossing the surface of the stone and often overlapping" [S4]. On the Awakening Slave "tool marks left by the sculptor's chisels and rasps make the marble almost into a picture surface" [S12]. The Atlas page lists a rasp among its tools [S21, summary]. Inferred for 3D: scratch fields under 0.5 mm deep, scratches 0.5 to 1 mm apart, in strokes 5 to 15 cm long crossing at random angles; they erase the claw grooves and leave a dull, even skin.

## Abrasives and polish: pumice, emery, tripoli, straw, leather

Stated, period. Vasari on the Pietrasanta marbles: the marble "is polished with pumice stone, with chalk from Tripoli, and with leather and wisps of straw" [S1]. In the sculpture chapter: "points of pumice stone" are rubbed over the surface for "a flesh-like appearance", "tripoli earth" adds lustre, and straw "made into bunches" is rubbed on for the final shine [S1]. Cellini: finish by polishing "with a fine white, close-grained pumice stone" [S2]. Vasari names emery and leather for porphyry, not for marble; he uses "smerigli" (emery veins) for the hard flaws in marble that break tools [S1]. Lead appears in Vasari only as fountain pipes [S1]; a lead lap is a hardstone and lapidary practice and is not documented for the David in anything read here.

Stated, general. Abrasives range "from harder ones, like emery, to softer ones, such as sandstone or pumice", applied wet so the water carries off the dust; polishing works from rough to fine; a high gloss is "extremely labour intensive"; Renaissance workers coated some Roman statues with wax, which "reflects the light in much the same way as a high gloss polish" and has since yellowed [S4].

Michelangelo. Stated: Michelangelo's finishing methods and materials went unrecorded [S23, summary]. The Taddei Tondo was left unpolished and unwaxed, unlike the David "when finished" [S15]. After the 2004 cleaning Franca Falletti said that in some areas "the original shine of the marble had even returned" [S24]. The Prisoners and St Matthew carry no abrasive finish at all.

Inferred for 3D. Pumice (a grey, hand-sized lump, worked wet) removes the rasp scratches and leaves a matt skin; tripoli (fine earth, with water, on a leather pad) raises a soft sheen; straw bunches and leather burnish to a satin finish, not a mirror gloss. A mirror gloss is a modern power-tool look and is not supported by the record.

## Measuring tools

Stated. Vasari: a carpenter's square (`squadra`) with one leg horizontal at the foot of the figure and the other upright, set against the block, with a matching square on the model so that projections of legs and arms can be transferred between model and stone [S1]. Cellini: draw the principal views on the block before cutting [S2]. The 1907 editor adds Alberti's `definitor` (a horizontal disc and plumb line, in Alberti's De statua), Diderot's square frames with plumb lines, and names the pointing machine as the modern aid [S1]. Compasses (`seste`) appear in Vasari for enlarging drawings, not in his marble chapter [S1]. Inferred: a plumb line, a large wooden square and a pair of iron compasses are enough to animate the layout stage; a pointing machine is anachronistic.

## Michelangelo's own evidence for the tools

Stated. The Louvre sheet with the David's arm and the "fromba / arco" line [S3] [S19, summary]. The Operai record of 9 September 1501 says he removed a "nodum" from the chest "uno vel duobus ictibus scarpelli", with one or two chisel blows [S25]. Vasari's story of the marble dust Michelangelo let fall from the scaffold planks to fool Soderini [S25] [S26]. A letter in the Milanesi edition (CCCLXIV) about "ferri dolci e buoni", soft good irons fetched from the ironworks by Donato, concerns lifting irons for the Seravezza columns, not chisels [S27, summary]. Wikipedia's claim that Michelangelo "made his steel chisels himself" rests on a geologist's article (Barron 2018) [S3]; none of the period texts read here says it. Caterina Rapetti's study holds that, judging from the unfinished works, he never gave up the tools and methods of the stonecutters and quarrymen [S28, summary].

## Dimensions this note is most and least sure of

Most sure, all stated by [S4] with a 2003 cross-check on the David [S3]: the point's shaft (20 to 30 cm, 1 to 2.5 cm); the claw's 3 to 5 teeth and 35 to 60 degree angle; the trace sizes on St Matthew (2 to 10 mm wide, 1 to 5 mm deep [S11]); the Italian hammer's square head and 15 to 20 cm handle; the point's 90 / 70 / 45 degree working angles.

Least sure, all inferred: the mallet weight (400 g to 1.5 kg is the whole modern spread); the drill bit diameters (no source gives one); the depth of a roughing furrow (no measurement exists, the Prisoners' photographs are the only guide); Vasari's drill weight of 12 to 20 pounds, whose meaning is unclear; whether Michelangelo used a wooden mallet at all.
